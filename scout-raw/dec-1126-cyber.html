Short News

Google Deepmind lets AI agents run experiments on their own for the next Gemini model. On the company's podcast, VP of Research Koray Kavukcuoglu, who leads Gemini development, frames this as part of a strategic shift at the lab. Rather than running separate teams for specialized projects like AlphaFold and nuclear fusion, Deepmind now relies on a single general-purpose algorithm for both science and products, he says. Each Gemini generation, he adds, is a hand-built one-off rather than a fixed architecture, while parallel teams work on its successors.

According to Kavukcuoglu, AI agents control part of the compute themselves to run experiments, though the lab is still far from full recursive self-improvement. As a safety measure, Deepmind doesn't reward the agents' internal thought streams ("Chain of Thought") during training, he says, so their intentions stay unfiltered and reviewable in natural language before they act.

Kavukcuoglu also points to limits. Today's AI can depict physical processes such as fluid flows and collisions with visual accuracy, he says, but doesn't understand them precisely. No test can show when models will close those gaps and reach artificial general intelligence (AGI), he argues, because no benchmark captures the core of intelligence. In his view, AGI won't begin until useful everyday products earn enough trust for people to hand decisions over to them, the way they would when working with other people.

Short News

Startup PrismML has released Ternary Bonsai 2.5 27B, an updated version of its aggressively compressed language model. It's based on Qwen3.8-27B and takes up just 5.9 GB of memory, same as its predecessor. That's possible because of ternary quantization, where each weight can only be one of three values (−1, 0, or +1) instead of the usual 16-bit storage. At full precision, a model this size would need about 54 GB.

Bonsai 2.5 is designed to perform more reliably on agentic tasks that involve long chains of tool calls. It needs fewer passes and tokens than Bonsai 2, and it's less likely to get stuck in loops or spiral into drawn-out reasoning phases.

The benchmark numbers reflect the improvement. On SWE-bench Verified, the score jumps from 60.8 to 75.6 percent. The full Qwen model hits 80.6. On AppWorld, an agent benchmark, Bonsai leaps from 51.2 to 90.5, landing just behind the base model. On LiveCodeBench v6, the new version scores slightly lower than its predecessor.

On an RTX 4090, the model generates about 204 tokens per second using multi-token prediction. The weights are available under an Apache 2.0 license and run via llama.cpp on Nvidia and Intel GPUs, as well as through MLX on Mac, iPhone, and iPad. PrismML grew out of a research team at Caltech and is backed by Khosla Ventures, Google, and Samsung, among others. The company first introduced Bonsai 27B in July, built on an older Qwen model. Its smallest variant needed less than 4 GB of memory, small enough to run directly on an iPhone.

MIT researchers have developed a method that makes training AI climate models faster, cheaper, and more reliable. According to MIT News, these emulators now need only a fraction of the data typically required.

Normally, researchers rely on massive, physics-based climate simulations that take months to run a single scenario on supercomputers. Fast AI models for climate prediction can mimic these in seconds and help plan power grids, public health measures, and agriculture. But training them previously required six to eight runs of those large simulations using evenly distributed, realistic future scenarios. That took so long the finished models were usually outdated, according to co-author Noelle Selin. They also often failed on unfamiliar situations.

An algorithm built by a team from MIT, ETH Zurich, and Imperial College London uses synthetic training data with extreme swings instead, like a sudden CO2 spike in 2050 or dropping methane in 2037. These targeted spikes help the AI understand the climate system far more precisely, according to lead author Christopher Womack. A single simulation run is enough for training, according to the team, and the AI reliably calculates the effects of local environmental policies too.

Short News

Anthropic now lets you install Claude Artifacts as Mac or Windows apps. The feature is in beta as of today and requires the latest version of Claude for macOS or Windows. You build an Artifact in chat and keep it in the dock without writing any code.

The installed apps can use OS APIs, connectors, and Claude itself. That lets an app add menu bar items, create transparent windows, and send notifications, among other things. Examples include custom terminals, file managers, and video editors. Several demos are available to try.

Anthropic's Felix Rieseberg wrote on X that you need a Claude account and the Claude app for now, though a free account is enough. If you have an Apple Developer account, you can also have Claude build a standalone app.

Vas Narasimhan, CEO of Swiss drugmaker Novartis and an Anthropic board member, is pushing back on expectations that AI could cure cancer in five to 10 years. That puts him at odds with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and chemistry Nobel laureate Demis Hassabis, who founded DeepMind and its drug discovery spinoff Isomorphic. Around Anthropic's IPO, Amodei suggested that cancer, Alzheimer's, heart disease, and mental illness could be cured in under a decade, while Hassabis said last year that AI could defeat all diseases within 10 years.

According to the Financial Times, Narasimhan points to a limited understanding of human biology, which is why lengthy animal and human trials will still be needed. His forecast is more cautious: within a decade, he expects AI to cut average drug development only from 12 years to eight, with the early phase before human trials alone accounting for up to two years of that. He also expects success rates in clinical development to rise from 8 percent to 12 percent.

Anthropic is investing in its own drug research, acquired biotech startup Coefficient Bio, and is building a biology lab. Isomorphic raised $5 billion last week and is working with pharmaceutical companies including Novartis on new drugs. Narasimhan sees AI's real benefit as "somewhere in the middle" between ambitious AI forecasts and a skeptical pharma industry.

Source: Financial Times / Novartis CEO on AI
